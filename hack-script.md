# Chalkboard D(a)emon — 5-minute pitch script

**Target:** 4:30–4:50. The event has a hard five-minute stop.  
**Style:** theatrical horror-comedy; play the warnings like a cursed product demo.  
**Safety note:** describe the screech as a concept, not a live demo at full volume. The room has suffered enough.

## Slide 1 — Chalkboard D(a)emon (0:20)
Hi everyone. I’m pitching Chalkboard D(a)emon: a brutally effective cure for brain rot. It is a daemon in your phone, laptop, PC, and wherever else you go to lose forty minutes to a video of a raccoon washing a grape.

It is also a demon. We are not being subtle about the product-market fit with hell.

## Slide 2 — The problem (0:20)
We all know the ritual. “Just five more minutes.” Then you look up and it’s tomorrow, your water is warm, and you have learned seventeen facts about a celebrity you do not like.

The feed is infinite. Your attention span is not.

## Slide 3 — The cure (0:25)
Our solution is social pressure, powered by an unnecessarily hostile machine. After a configurable threshold—let’s say twenty minutes of doomscrolling—Chalkboard D(a)emon decides you have sinned.

And unlike your screen-time notification, it does not politely ask whether you would like to ignore it.

## Slide 4 — Why daemon? (0:20)
A daemon is a background program: software that quietly keeps running without you opening it every time. Ours watches for the scroll spiral across your devices.

Phone. Laptop. PC. Anywhere the algorithm has established a beachhead.

## Slide 5 — Why demon? (0:25)
Because when you invoke it, it calls upon the seven circles of hell and the multi-millennia fury of the undead whose souls rest uneasy.

You have spent twenty minutes scrolling. Now you, too, shall rest uneasy.

## Slide 6 — How it works (0:35)
The daemon notices that you have been on a social app for too long. It sends an alert to an ESP32 device. The device says your name, names your sins—meaning the app you have been browsing—and then begins the punishment.

The default threshold is twenty minutes, but it is configurable. We are monsters, not unreasonable monsters.

## Slide 7 — The hardware (0:25)
The device has a speaker for the voice of judgement, a servo, a metal scribe, and a metal plate. The servo drags the scribe across the plate, producing the sound of a haunted classroom and a thousand chalkboards dying at once.

This is the part where a normal person would use a notification. We rejected normality.

## Slide 8 — Example (0:25)
Imagine your room is peaceful. You are on TikTok. Twenty minutes pass.

[Deliver as an announcement.] “LYRA! YOU HAVE SINNED ON TIKTOK FOR TWENTY MINUTES! PREPARE THYSELF!”

[Pause.] Then: [mime one short, awful screech].

If nobody looks at you, the device has failed to reach its target audience. So it keeps screeching until you stop.

## Slide 9 — Why it works (0:25)
The genius is that the punishment is not just noise. It is social. You are now the person who summoned the demon in a shared room. Your flatmates, classmates, coworkers, and possibly the barangay all know what you were doing.

The product converts private procrastination into public consequences. Horrible. Efficient. Memorable.

## Slide 10 — Seven circles (0:20)
The circles are Denial, Distraction, Obsession, Dissociation, Rage, Emptiness, and You. Every circle is a feed. The final circle is your For You page.

Dante could never have imagined the algorithmic horrors we have built for ourselves.

## Slide 11 — Privacy and control (0:25)
A real version should do as much detection locally as possible, let users choose which apps count, offer quiet hours, and include a physical kill switch. We are pitching a silly device, not a surveillance startup.

The goal is to interrupt the scroll—not to collect a dossier of your sins.

## Slide 12 — The ask (0:20)
This is a pitch, not a working prototype. We need no code tonight; we need your imagination and your votes. The implementation would pair a cross-device background service with a small ESP32 sound device and a mechanically awful sound generator.

## Slide 13 — The catch (0:15)
And the rule is simple: it will not stop unless you stop. You can close the app, put the phone down, or continue to be personally victimized by a metal plate.

## Slide 14 — Closing (0:15)
Chalkboard D(a)emon. Because sometimes the only way to stop brain rot is to make everyone around you hate you.

May all your souls be damned—lest one stops the doom of endless scrolls. Thank you.
